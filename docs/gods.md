# Gods and rites

Checked against the Populous manuals, not memory. The rites in Hearthmarch are original. They borrow the structure of those games: worship pays for power, cheaper works come first, and you spend the power by pointing at the land.

## What the manuals actually say

**Populous (1989).** The Electronic Arts manual says a deity's power comes from followers: "The more followers you have and the greater their achievements, the more power you wield." The Manna Bar is a row of divine interventions. Raise and Lower Land, on the far left, "requires the least manna," and Armageddon, on the far right, "requires the most." A marker shows what you can invoke: any command to the left of it. Gathering manna moves the marker right; using a command moves it left. The manual's own questions section answers why the marker climbs slowly: "The more followers you have worshipping you, the larger your manna source. You can increase your manna by increasing your population and increasing the technology of your settlements." Bigger settlements produce more manna than huts. The interventions include raising and lowering land, placing the Papal Magnet, earthquake (the close-up area is shaken, buildings fall, people drown), swamp (flat land; anyone who falls in drowns), knight (the leader becomes an autonomous fighter and you lose that leader), volcano, flood (the sea rises by one), and armageddon.

**Populous II: Trials of the Olympian Gods.** The manual's Mana Gauge "measures your Mana. Mana is a reservoir of power you can use to do Effects. Mana comes from the pious worship of your followers — the more worshippers you have, the greater your Mana." A later section is plainer: "You earn mana from your populations divine offerings, so increase your population as quickly as you can." Effects cost different amounts. "A lesser effect like lowering and raising land costs little." "Great effects like tidal waves and earthquakes are expensive." You pick an effect, then click the close-up map. There are six elements — People, Vegetation, Earth, Air, Fire, and Water — and experience spent on an element makes that element's effects stronger. Influence orders such as settle and fight cost no mana. Swamp is laid on flat ground and swallows walkers. Earthquake shakes a patch. Volcano ruins the land under it. Flood raises the sea.

The manuals do not publish a numeric cost table. Hearthmarch uses its own numbers.

## How that sits on this marsh

Hearthmarch already trades tax for villagers. Villagers are the worshippers. Faith is the manna bar:

- Each dawn adds faith from the current population, scaled down by the tax. At 0% the villages pray in full. At 30% they add no faith. Gold and faith pull in opposite directions, the same way gold and population already do.
- A kingdom starts with enough faith for one of each of its three rites. Spending drops the bowl. It refills only while the tax leaves people in the villages.
- Each rite has a cost and a short rest, so a click cannot fire twice. The great rite costs the most, in the spirit of the manna bar's right-hand end, without copying those effects.
- You choose the god before the march. That god is the whole set of rites, the way Populous II spends a deity's experience on one family of effects. Reedmoor keeps a different god and calls rites on its own.
- A rite is aimed: click the rite, then click a tile. Hearths cannot be sunk or scorched away. Wounds stop at 1 strength. The match is still won by taking the other hearth.

The three gods are Lark (open grass), Kiln (stone and ash), and Mere (still water). Their rites reshape meadow, wood, and water, or steady and shake marchers. Nothing here uses Populous's names, magnets, or knights.
